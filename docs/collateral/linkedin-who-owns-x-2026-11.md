## POST A — Takealot  (personal profile, Mon)

Most people think Amazon owns Takealot. Some guess Prosus. Both are wrong.

South Africa's biggest online store is owned by Naspers — the Cape Town tech group famous for its early Tencent bet. Held directly, and now almost entirely.

The timeline is the real story:

2011 — Kim Reid (ex-MWEB) and Tiger Global relaunch a small e-tailer as takealot.com
2015 — Naspers takes 46.5%, folding in its own Kalahari.com
2018 — Naspers lifts that to 96%
2026 — Takealot posts its first-ever annual profit

So after 15 years and a long cash burn, the asset finally turns a profit — and the upside sits in Cape Town, not Seattle or Amsterdam.

The headline question is who owns it. The one that decides the return is who kept the value: who authored it, who owns the apparatus that prices it, who walks away with the margin once it works.

## POST B — MTN  (personal profile, Wed)

Who owns MTN — Africa's biggest mobile network? No founder. No foreign parent. The single largest owner is a state pension fund.

MTN Group is JSE-listed with no controlling shareholder — a genuinely wide free float. The biggest single holder isn't a telecoms giant or a founding family. It's the Public Investment Corporation, the state-owned manager of South Africa's government pension fund.

So the largest economic claim on Africa's largest mobile operator traces back to millions of South African government pensioners — not a parent company in Paris or London.

That's rarer than it sounds. Most of the continent's marquee assets have a controlling hand somewhere: a family, a multinational, a founder. MTN doesn't. Which raises the question that actually decides the return — when no one controls the cap table, who captures the value the network throws off, and who's accountable for it?

The full cap table is here:

https://www.monokromatik.com/article/who-owns-mtn

Did you know a pension fund was MTN's #1 shareholder?

## POST C — Pick n Pay  (personal profile, Fri)

A single family controlled Pick n Pay for 60 years. In the last 18 months, they've quietly let go.

South Africa's storied grocer is JSE-listed, but the Ackerman family long ran it through a high-voting share structure. That grip is unwinding fast:

2024 — a rescue rights issue; the family cuts its vote from 52% to 49% and gives up the right to name the chair, CEO and CFO
Nov 2025 — the family sells about 8.5% for roughly R1.6bn; high-voting 'B' shares are cancelled, dropping its voting control to 36.8% and its economic stake to 18.2%

After six decades, Pick n Pay is passing to its public register — institutions and index funds, not a founding family.

The headline is who owns it now. The sharper question is what a founder-family actually hands over when it finally lets go: the brand, yes — but did it keep the apparatus that captures the value, or is that passing too?
