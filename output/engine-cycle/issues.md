## Issue 002 — Culture Is Business: Who Owns the Upside

**Theme:** Who Owns the Upside

**Editorial argument (one paragraph):**
Issue 001 argued that the world keeps discovering African value late and converting it into someone else's case study. Issue 002 reports what happened next: in late 2025 and into 2026, the global infrastructure of culture — management companies, development banks, sports-rights machines, luxury distributors, sportswear houses — moved into African culture deliberately and at scale. Hybe, the company behind BTS, took its first African artist. Afreximbank stood up a US$1bn Africa Film Fund. CAF turned AFCON Morocco into the most commercially successful tournament in African football history, with revenues up over 90% and 23 sponsors. None of this is hype; it is capital and structure arriving. The strategic question MonoKromatik exists to answer is therefore no longer *does Africa have value* but *who owns the infrastructure that captures it* — the management contract, the fund's cap table, the broadcast rights, the retail licence, the IP. This issue reads six current stories through that single lens: where the upside is being built, and who is positioned to keep it.

**Feature slate (6):**

1. **COVER ESSAY — Who Owns the Upside.** The framing argument: 2026 is the year the debate shifts from visibility to ownership. Grounded in the convergence of the Hybe–Tyla deal, the $1bn film fund and AFCON's commercial record — three signals that global structure has arrived and the contest is now over who holds the infrastructure.

2. **THE BOARDROOM / THE BACKROOM — Hybe Takes Tyla: A Global Machine Meets an African Career.** Premise: Hybe (the BTS company) formed a JV, NFO LLC, and a global management partnership with Grammy-winner Tyla — its first African artist — extending Bang Si-hyuk's 'multi-home, multi-genre' strategy. Who gains scale, who keeps creative control, and what it signals about who manages African stardom.

3. **THE WORK — Afreximbank's US$1bn Film Fund: Financing the Story or Owning It?** Premise: A source-led read of the CANEX/FEDA Africa Film Fund (launched Kigali, May 2025; production, post and distribution) against Nigeria's 'Destination 2030' $100bn creative-export ambition — does continental capital finally back African IP, or recreate old ownership patterns?

4. **WILL IT LAND? — AFCON's Commercial Record: Can the Football Economy Translate Beyond the Tournament?** Premise: AFCON Morocco 2025 drove a 90%+ revenue surge, 23 sponsors and a 61% global-viewership rise via the CAF–IMG partnership. The relevance test: does this prove a durable African sports-marketing economy, or a host-and-rights-holder windfall that doesn't reach clubs and players?

5. **BRAND WEATHER — The Luxury Frontier: Lagos, Nairobi, Johannesburg and the Distributor Workaround.** Premise: A briefing on global luxury's African entry — rising affluent households, but still almost no LVMH/Kering/Richemont monobrand stores, with access intermediated by distributors (Aksal, Apsley) and homegrown retail (Alara, Temple Muse, Little Red). The signal: who controls the shelf controls the margin.

6. **CULTURE — Faces of the House: Tyla, Burna Boy and the Endorsement Economy.** Premise: African artists as global campaign faces — Tyla with Nike, Pandora and Hybe; Burna Boy with BOSS and On. A read on whether ambassadorship is building artist-owned equity or renting African cool by the campaign.
