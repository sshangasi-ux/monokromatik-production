# **"THE INFRASTRUCTURE ISSUE"**
### Cover line: *Culture built the room. Now who owns the building?*

---

## Editorial Thesis

**[INTERPRETIVE]** Africa's creative economy is at a structural inflection point. The first phase — cultural visibility, streaming virality, diaspora pride — has already been won. The second phase is harder and more consequential: converting cultural dominance into durable, African-owned economic infrastructure. The timing of this issue is deliberate. 
Brookings estimates Africa's creative economy could reach $200 billion by 2030, with music, film, and digital content becoming scalable export assets — *if* infrastructure and policy align.
 That conditional clause — *if* — is our editorial centre of gravity. 
Africa's creative industries have become one of the continent's most influential growth sectors, yet Africa still captures only a small fraction of the economic value tied to its creativity.
 Across music, fashion, tech, and diaspora capital, the same structural gap recurs: the culture ships, the money doesn't follow home. This issue maps who is actively building the pipes — rights frameworks, payment rails, luxury institutions, IP policy — and who is still waiting for a seat at someone else's table.

**Confidence:** Interpretive — the structural argument is original editorial synthesis grounded in citable trends.

### Feature 01 — ROOTS Franchise
# **The IP Reckoning: Who Owns African Music When the Money Gets Real?**

**The angle:** Afrobeats has posted the numbers. 
The Sub-Saharan Africa music industry is the fastest-growing worldwide, with revenue up over 20 percent in 2025 alone according to Spotify's Sub-Saharan Africa Managing Director.
 
Live Nation reports that US Afrobeats shows are up 400% year-on-year.
 But the distribution of that revenue tells a different story: 
global recorded music revenue hit nearly $30 billion in 2024, of which Sub-Saharan Africa's share is less than 0.4%.
 The structural problem is not talent — it is ownership. 
Asset-based financing — long used in traditional sectors — is now making sense for creative industries; intellectual property such as music catalogues produce predictable cash flows when structured properly.
 This feature investigates the gap between cultural output and economic capture in African music IP: unclaimed royalties, catalogue ownership, rights fragmentation, and the policy moves designed to close it.

**Why it earns a place:** Music is MonoKromatik's most-cited sector for diaspora readers. The IP-as-infrastructure framing takes the conversation upstream from artist profiles to structural economics.

**Candidate subjects:**
- **Mavin Global / Universal Music Group deal** as a case study: 
Universal Music Group acquired a majority stake in Mavin Global, valued at $150–200 million, with Mavin's roster achieving a combined 6 billion streams in 2023
 — interrogate what "majority stake" means for catalogue control long-term.
- **Phylis Atieno's November 2025 report** *"Royalties, Rights, and Returns"* on unclaimed African royalties as a structural opportunity — candidate for interview.
- **Nigeria's updated IP policy and strategy document** as a government counterweight: 
governments including Nigeria, Ghana, Uganda, and Botswana are reworking policies, signalling growing recognition that IP is not just legal protection but economic infrastructure.

- CISAC data anchor: 
CISAC's Global Collections Report 2025 shows creators' royalties at a record €13.97 billion in 2024, with digital income growing double-digits.


**Confidence:** Verified (data points); Interpretive (structural framing).

### Feature 02 — ARENA Franchise
# **The Ownership Gap: African Fashion's Infrastructure Problem**

**The angle:** African fashion has achieved cultural legitimacy. 
If 2025 marked a year of expansion — designers embracing quiet luxury, craft revival, and bold prints — 2026 is the year the continent sharpens those ideas into something more assertive.
 
The demand for African textiles and prints has driven Western and Asian luxury brands to seek out African partnerships, with over $15 billion in African exports of textile, clothing, and footwear annually.
 Yet cultural influence is not translating into institutional ownership. 
While African culture shapes global industries, ownership and long-term economic value often remain concentrated elsewhere — in fashion and luxury especially, African aesthetics continue to influence global design, often without sustained returns flowing back to the communities connected to those traditions.
 The feature interrogates the production-to-ownership gap: who is building the infrastructure — manufacturing capacity, luxury retail, institutional archives — to hold the gains?

**Why it earns a place:** Fashion is a lead franchise for MonoKromatik. The piece shifts the conversation from runway aesthetics to supply-chain economics and institutional architecture.

**Candidate subjects:**
- **ÀLKÉ** — newly launched institution explicitly designed to convert African cultural influence into long-term economic value: 
ÀLKÉ's model is built around three structures, beginning with an annual Ball as a convening platform for creatives, investors, and policymakers, with the inaugural edition in Cape Town and future editions rotating to Lagos, Nairobi, Dakar, Accra, Addis Ababa, and Cairo.

- **Thebe Magugu** (South Africa) and **Iamisigo** (Nigeria) — designers navigating international awards circuits while confronting 
the reality that not every African designer is ready to enter the international market due to gaps in infrastructure, production, and manufacturing capacity to withstand a surge in international orders.

- **Meta AI x I.N. Official**: 
Meta AI sponsored Lagos-based brand I.N. Official's Spring/Summer 2026 show in London — something that had never been seen before, a tech company sponsoring an African designer's collection.
 What does Big Tech patronage mean for creative independence?
- **The AfCFTA angle**: 
The African Continental Free Trade Area is predicted to increase intra-African textile trade by 33%
 — ask which designers are positioned to benefit.

**Confidence:** Verified (data points); Interpretive (the ownership-gap framing is editorial synthesis).

### Feature 03 — WAVES Franchise
# **The Diaspora Stack: How African Founders Are Building the Financial Infrastructure the Continent Needs**

**The angle:** A new generation of diaspora-founded fintechs has discovered a durable playbook: build for the gap between continent and diaspora, then use hard-currency diaspora revenue to fund continental operations. 
NALA used diaspora revenue to fund the parallel development of Rafiki, its B2B payment infrastructure play — the diaspora business funds the continental business, and global expansion generates the hard-currency revenue that makes African operations more fundable.
 This is not remittance sentimentality; it is a capital strategy. 
Diaspora innovators are uniquely positioned to blend technical expertise, global networks, and cultural intelligence — they understand the pain points of Africans abroad and the opportunities of African markets at home.
 The feature profiles the founders executing this dual-market architecture and asks whether it is a sustainable model or a transitional bridge.

**Why it earns a place:** The fintech-diaspora nexus is the most commercially significant story in African business right now. WAVES covers culture-commerce convergence — this feature anchors it in structural finance.

**Candidate subjects:**
- **NALA / Benjamin Fernandes**: 
Profitable and handling remittances into 11 African markets, NALA secured $40 million in 2024; Fernandes targets 2026 positioning Rafiki as the primary payout network for global remittance firms tapping Africa's $100 billion annual diaspora flows.

- **Moniepoint / Tosin Eniolorunda**: 
Moniepoint acquired Bancom Europe in the UK in July 2025, securing an electronic money institution licence covering the European Economic Area, with the strategic logic being the Nigeria-Europe remittance corridor.

- **LemFi**: 
London-based neobank founded by Nigerian diaspora entrepreneurs, LemFi closed a $53 million Series B in January 2025, led by Highland Europe.

- **Kredete**: 
Tackling the problem that immigrants arrive with no credit history, Kredete allows users to send money home using stablecoin-backed rails while simultaneously building a US credit score — a dual model linking diaspora financial obligations to financial advancement.

- **Counter-angle**: 
The count of US-based investors in African startup deals dropped from over 30 in early 2025 to about 14 in early 2026 — a decline of roughly 53%.
 Ask whether the diaspora infrastructure model is structurally resilient when global VC retreats.

### Feature 04 — THE INDEX Franchise
# **Debt Is the New Equity: Reading Africa's 2026 Funding Shift**

**The angle:** The MonoKromatik Index tracks numbers that move culture. Right now, the most consequential number in African business is a ratio. 
The most significant structural shift in African startup financing over the past 18 months is that debt has overtaken equity — in Q1 2026, debt and hybrid financing accounted for approximately $490 million of the $705 million tracked, roughly 70% of total capital, with pure equity funding at just $212 million.
 
Equity capital fell from 76% of total funding in early 2025 to 43% in early 2026, while debt capital rose from 24% to 57% — a 165% increase.
 This is not a funding winter — 
African startups raised between $597 million and $705 million in Q1 2026 alone, representing a 27% year-on-year increase over Q1 2025.
 It is a maturation: founders keeping ownership, investors demanding cash flow. The Index feature decodes what this shift means for who gets built, who gets bypassed, and which sectors will define the next cycle.

**Why it earns a place:** The Index is MonoKromatik's data-narrative franchise. This piece gives readers a framework for interpreting every funding headline they see for the next 12 months.

**Candidate subjects / data nodes:**
- 
A major trend in early 2026 is the dominance of climatetech — while most tech deals are traditionally equity-based, there is a massive shift toward debt funding in this sector
, because infrastructure-heavy startups can scale hardware without surrendering ownership.
- 
African startups raised $575 million across 58 deals between January and February 2026, with logistics, transport, and energy startups capturing a growing share of the capital.

- The **gender gap as a structural indicator**: 
women-led startups received less than 10% of total funding in Q1 2026.

- 
Approximately 83% of startup funding in Q1 2025 was secured by startups in just four countries — Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and Egypt
 — flag the concentration risk and identify which next-tier markets (Morocco, Rwanda, Ghana) are gaining ground.
- Interview candidates: a debt-instrument specialist at a DFI (IFC, BII); a founder who chose debt over equity and why.

**Confidence:** Verified.

### Feature 05 — ROOTS Franchise (Long Read)
# **Brand Africa's Blind Spot: Why African Brands Keep Losing the Continent to Foreign Labels**

**The angle:** The Brand Africa survey has tracked what Africans most admire across 30 countries for more than a decade. The finding is consistent and, for this publication, urgently editorial: 
non-African brands hold an average 80% share of Africa's most admired brands — and African brands peaked at 34% admiration in 2011, falling to a historic low of 11% in 2025.
 This is not a cultural defeat — it is a brand-infrastructure deficit. 
Only five African brands have appeared in every edition since the survey began — Dangote, Glo, MTN, Tiger Brands, and Tusker — and they are products of sustained, disciplined, culturally intelligent marketing investment.
 This long read asks what it would take to rebuild African brand share — through the lens of marketing leadership, cultural intelligence, and the institutions capable of sustaining it.

**Why it earns a place:** Brand equity is MonoKromatik's core intellectual territory. This piece is both provocation and brief — asking readers to think about brand-building as infrastructure investment, not aesthetics.

**Candidate subjects:**
- 
The inaugural ACMO 100 list, produced by Brand Africa, names African marketing leaders building the most powerful brands in Africa and beyond — the first comprehensive record of its kind after 15 years of Brand Africa research.

- 
Eight diaspora leaders based in the US and UAE — running marketing at Visa, Unilever, Doordash, and BET Media Group — underscore the mobility of African-origin talent operating at the top of the world's most competitive brand portfolios.
 Commission one of them for a first-person perspective piece.
- **The creative economy as a brand-building flywheel**: 
African creative businesses are tasked with staying authentic — sourcing locally, telling authentic African stories, and showcasing heritage drives employment, strengthens supply chains, and fuels sustainable growth.

- Counter-voices: ask whether "African brand" is the right unit of analysis or whether pan-African brand platforms require continental infrastructure (AfCFTA, harmonised IP) that does not yet exist at scale.

**Confidence:** Verified (Brand Africa data); Interpretive (the infrastructure diagnosis is editorial).
