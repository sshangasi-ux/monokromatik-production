## Conversations brief: Bernice Samuels on what a brand actually does

- **Format:** interview brief
- **Confidence:** verified
- **Needs human approval:** YES
- **Angle:** The continent's most decorated corporate marketer is exiting MTN in 2026 — the rare moment a brand custodian will speak with nothing left to sell.

## Who
Bernice Samuels — MTN Group Executive for Brand and Marketing (since January 2017), retiring in 2026 after more than three decades across telecoms, brewing, financial services, broadcasting and technology. Earlier roles include CMO of MTN South Africa and CMO of FNB. Her career has shaped brand strategy across MTN's ~23 markets, and she is associated with the maxim 'a brand is as a brand does.' Recognition includes Marketer of the Year (2012 and 2021), the Financial Mail AdFocus Lifetime Achiever Award (2020), and an inaugural place in the Baobab | ACMO Hall of Fame.

Profile/source: https://www.mtn.com/mtns-bernice-samuels-interviewed-by-african-business/ and https://theorg.com/org/mtn-group-ltd/org-chart/bernice-samuels

## Why now
A 30-year custodian of one of Africa's largest brands is stepping down in the same year the African marketing industry is openly debating whether 'purpose-led' brand-building survives an AI-and-efficiency squeeze. A retiring executive can be candid in a way a sitting CMO cannot — about what continental brand-building got wrong, what 'localisation' really cost, and whether African brands are still being measured by foreign yardsticks.

## Proposed questions
1. You're associated with 'a brand is as a brand does.' Across 23 markets, where did MTN's actions and its brand promise come apart — and what did you change because of it?
2. African marketing in 2026 talks about purpose and 'viscerality.' From inside a Pan-African operator, where is purpose real strategy and where is it a slide?
3. What did building one brand across 23 markets teach you that a single-market CMO can never learn — and what doesn't translate across borders?
4. If you were briefing a 28-year-old African marketer today, what advice from your own career would you now retract?
5. What's the one investment in brand you fought for and lost — and were you right?

- https://theorg.com/org/mtn-group-ltd/org-chart/bernice-samuels

## Conversations brief: Godwin Tom on building the rails under Afrobeats

- **Format:** interview brief
- **Confidence:** verified
- **Needs human approval:** YES
- **Angle:** Not the artist, the infrastructure: the operator who turned music-business education into a continental institution as Afrobeats' global moment outpaces its plumbing.

## Who
Godwin Tom — Nigerian talent manager and music executive with two decades in the industry; founder of what began in 2017 as an internship programme, became the Music Business Academy for Africa (MBA for Africa, formalised 2020), and in December 2025 was rebranded the Creative Business Academy for Africa, widening scope from music into fashion and film. The programme reports having trained more than 1,500 people, with curriculum developed in partnership with Dr Carlos Chirinos of New York University. Tom also launched a Women Fund to address gender underrepresentation in the industry.

Profile/source: https://godwintom.com/ , https://cbaforafrica.org/about-us/ , https://www.readcommunique.com/p/mba-for-africa-creative-academy-pivot

## Why now
Afrobeats has the global stage (Grammys, stadium tours, Move Afrika-style touring infrastructure) but the recurring 2026 story is structural: who owns the catalogues, who trains the managers, who keeps the value on the continent. Tom is one of the few operators building the unglamorous layer — education, management capacity, gender equity — rather than chasing the spotlight. The rebrand from 'music' to 'creative business' is itself a thesis worth interrogating.

## Proposed questions
1. You renamed the academy from music to 'creative business' in late 2025. What did you see that made 'music' too narrow a frame?
2. Afrobeats won the world's attention before it built its institutions. Which missing piece of infrastructure worries you most in 2026?
3. You've trained over 1,500 people — what's the most common wrong assumption they arrive with about how this industry pays?
4. The Women Fund responds to a real gap. What changed in who applies, and what hasn't changed yet?
5. Catalogue ownership is the live debate. What does 'keeping the value on the continent' demand of managers, not just artists?

**Sources:**
- https://godwintom.com/
- https://cbaforafrica.org/about-us/
- https://www.readcommunique.com/p/mba-for-africa-creative-academy-pivot
- https://www.musicinafrica.net/directory/godwin-tom

## Conversations brief: Osayi Endolyn on diaspora storytelling as its own discipline

- **Format:** interview brief
- **Confidence:** verified
- **Needs human approval:** YES
- **Angle:** An award-winning diaspora food-and-culture writer who now publishes independently — a clear-eyed read on authorship, ownership and who gets to tell Black stories in 2026.

## Who
Osayi Endolyn — writer and publisher focused on African diaspora narratives, Black American culinary traditions and identity-centred storytelling. James Beard Award winner (Commercial Media, for the Hulu/Uber Eats series 'On the Rise') and James Beard nominee (U.S. Foodways, for 'The Rise'); co-author with Marcus Samuelsson of 'The Rise: Black Cooks and the Soul of American Food,' and a contributor to 'Black Power Kitchen' (Ghetto Gastro). She now writes the independent newsletter TEXTURE — long-form essays on culture, food, books and politics — which is actively publishing in 2026.

Profile/source: https://osayiendolyn.com and https://www.texture.news/

## Why now
The 2026 creator-economy story is dominated by monetisation, AI and scale — but the quieter shift is writers leaving institutional platforms to own their distribution directly. Endolyn sits exactly there: a decorated writer who built credibility inside major publishers and is now running her own publication. She is well placed to talk about authorship and ownership of Black/diaspora stories, what independence buys (and costs) a cultural critic, and where AI-generated 'African' content fits — or doesn't.

## Proposed questions
1. You've published inside major institutions and now run TEXTURE independently. What can you say in your own publication that you couldn't before?
2. You've written on Black stories being co-authored by white writers. In 2026, with AI now generating 'African' content, how has that question changed?
3. Food is your entry point but rarely your real subject. What does the table let you say about diaspora identity that a straight essay can't?
4. For a continent's worth of writers eyeing independence, what does going solo actually cost — beyond the institutional paycheque?
5. What's a diaspora story you think keeps getting told wrong, and how would you tell it?

**Sources:**
- https://osayiendolyn.com
- https://www.texture.news/
